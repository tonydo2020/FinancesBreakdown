How we calculate

The assumptions behind every episode, in one place.

Each episode runs a month-by-month simulation for one hypothetical person, from today until they reach financial independence or turn 70. These are the rules it follows.

Financial independence

Someone is financially independent when their invested savings reach 25 times their yearly spending. That comes from the 4% rule: historically, withdrawing 4% a year, adjusted for inflation, lasted at least 30 years in most market periods studied. All amounts are shown in today's dollars.

Market assumptions

ItemAssumption
Investment returns7% a year, before inflation. Some episodes also test a weaker 5% case.
Inflation3% a year
Savings account yield3.5% a year
Withdrawal rate4% (25 times yearly spending)
Prime rateRefreshed monthly from the Federal Reserve. Debt at twice prime or more counts as high-interest.

Taxes

Federal income tax uses the current year's IRS brackets and standard deduction, plus Social Security and Medicare. State income tax uses an approximate flat rate for the person's state. Contribution limits for 401(k)s, IRAs and HSAs are the current IRS limits.

The order of steps

Money is assigned in the same seven steps every time, based on the r/financialindependence flowchart: budget, emergency fund, high-interest debt, HSA, IRA, 401(k) (always taking the full employer match), then a taxable brokerage account.

Staying vs switching jobs

  • Staying: raises of inflation plus 1% a year.
  • Typical switcher: a new job every 2 years with about a 7% raise, in line with ADP and Bank of America data on job changers.
  • Negotiator: every 2 years for 15%. People report this online, but it isn't guaranteed.
  • Smart switcher: waits for the 401(k) vesting date, or negotiates a sign-on bonus that covers the match left behind.

Unvested employer match is forfeited at every switch, using realistic vesting schedules: 44% of plans vest immediately (PSCA), the rest use cliff or graded schedules. Switching stops after three moves.

Every episode is educational and hypothetical. It shows one reasonable way to run the numbers, not a recommendation for anyone's own money.

Sources

Every statistic quoted in an episode is listed, with its source, in that video's description. Common sources include the IRS, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Federal Reserve (G.19 consumer credit, SHED survey), the Atlanta Fed Wage Growth Tracker, ADP Pay Insights, Vanguard's How America Saves, Fidelity retirement data, PSCA plan surveys and the Census Bureau. Examples from online communities are paraphrased and anonymized.
